# USAA
*P&C Insurance Modernization*

---

## The Problem

USAA's digital products were built in the early 2000s and it showed. Mobile insurance shopping was growing fast, acquisition was leaking, and members were calling support for things they should have been able to do themselves. Meanwhile, Lemonade and its fintech cousins were making insurance feel easy — fast, mobile-first, built for a generation that didn't want to talk to anyone.

USAA still had the trust advantage. The experience wasn't holding up its end.

---

## My Role

Lead designer on P&C insurance. I sat between the team and our Director — defining project strategy, running workshops, leading stakeholder meetings, mentoring junior designers, and making the work happen. My Director handled the org layer. I handled everything in the room.

---

## The Users

USAA's membership was quietly shifting. The military core — active and former service members — was giving way to their families: spouses, kids, a generation that had never served and didn't carry the same institutional loyalty or privacy concerns. That created a genuine tension. Service members wanted formality and discretion. Their families wanted a normal consumer app. We had to modernize the experience without making longtime members feel like the brand had forgotten them.

Then there were the military-specific edge cases that weren't really edge cases: deployments, relocations, a spouse managing everything solo. These scenarios showed up constantly and the product mostly ignored them.

---

## The Process

Started with a heuristic assessment and usability testing to get an honest baseline — no redesigning before we knew what was actually broken. Ran a design sprint to align stakeholders on KPIs, size up the competition, and get testable directions on the table fast.

The tech modernization added a wrinkle: the new platform wasn't ready, but conversion was bleeding now. So we ran two tracks. Legacy A/B tests — built on the old stack, informed by new research — shipped quickly and hit the near-term KPIs while the full redesign continued in the background. We weren't waiting 18 months to show results.

Alongside all of that: led a service blueprint effort across a dozen business teams, mapping employee actions, regulatory requirements, and backend systems together for the first time. Regulatory constraints were driving a surprising amount of call center behavior and nobody had documented it. I built the process to be repeatable, trained other designers and product owners to run it themselves, and handed it off. It scaled without dedicated headcount because it was designed to.

---

## The Key Decision

Running legacy improvements and the full redesign in parallel instead of sequentially. It would've been easier to wait for the new stack. Instead we pulled insights from the redesign work and applied them immediately to production tests — delivering measurable wins throughout rather than asking the business to wait for a big reveal.

---

## What Was Hard

Four months into a renters insurance overhaul — research done, concepts tested, final approach nearly dev-ready — our primary stakeholder got reorganized out. New exec, new team, different ideas. They hired a third-party agency to redo the work and handed us an advisory role for the next twelve months.

The agency's final output was nearly identical to ours.

Managing that moment was more about leadership than design. The work was gone. The team felt it. Keeping people motivated while chaperon-ing someone else's version of your own project is a specific kind of hard. We did it. Leadership noticed. Steps were taken.

---

## Outcomes

- 4–6% conversion lift across P&C products; Homeowners up 5%
- Support calls down significantly for basic self-service actions
- Service blueprint process handed off and running across the org without us
- Mobile insurance redesign shipped in under 3 months, test-validated
- Hit near-term KPI targets in production while the full redesign was still in progress

---

## What I'd Do Differently

Start the service blueprint work earlier — run it alongside the initial assessment instead of after it. The ecosystem mapping uncovered regulatory dependencies and call center gaps that affected decisions already in flight. Knowing the operational layer sooner would've sharpened the problem definition from day one.
